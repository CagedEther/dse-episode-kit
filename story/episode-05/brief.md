## notes

About 110 seconds. Voice-over only. No on-screen dialogue.

Leave out: how she solves the review bottleneck. The episode names the problem and stops; the answer belongs to a later episode. Do not explain protocols, tooling, or how the agents are built. No weapons, gadgets, or action on screen. The spy crew is an analogy carried by the narration and the staging, not a literal heist.

Tone: playful spy-drama pastiche. The narration borrows the rhythm of a "putting the crew together" montage: each expert introduced in one line, a beat of tension, a handler making the call. Underneath, it stays plain about how the work is actually split.

## brief

Follows: the series lead, who runs a small studio. The narration calls her "she" and does not name her.

Opening hook: The client's new website has to be live by morning. She has one AI assistant trying to do all of it at once: decide the pages, write the words, design the look, and judge whether it works. The page on her screen is a little of everything and good at nothing. She shuts the draft.

Setup: Every spy drama has the moment the handler lays out the files and says this job needs a crew. Nobody sends one agent in to do everything. There is the sniper, the one who sees the whole field. The one who gets past the locks. The one who talks their way into the room. Complex AI work is the same. A big project goes better when it is broken into parts and each part goes to a specialist. Those specialists are sub-agents. She decides to build her crew.

Development: She assembles four sub-agents, introduced one at a time in the crew-montage style. They are drawn as people, like the agents in episode 01, each with one visual mark and no names on screen.
- The Architect maps the site: which pages exist, what goes on each, and the one path a visitor must be able to follow. She is the one who sees the whole field.
- The Copywriter writes every line the visitor reads. She works only inside the Architect's map.
- The Designer gives the pages their look and decides what the eye lands on first.
- The Evaluator does not build anything. She reads each finished page as a stranger would, and reports what fails.
She hands the job down the line. The map goes to the Copywriter, the words go to the Designer, and the pages go to the Evaluator. By midnight the site exists, and it looks better than anything the single assistant made.

Turn: The Evaluator fails the home page. It is beautiful, but a first-time visitor cannot find the one thing the client needs them to do. The Architect had marked it as the path that mattered most. Somewhere between the map and the design, that priority was lost. The Designer's version is the stronger picture. The Evaluator's note is the stronger page. The crew cannot settle it among themselves. Like every handler, she is the one who makes the call. She sends the page back with the path restored.

Cliffhanger: Her call works. The crew takes the fix and starts moving at speed. The Architect maps new sections, the Copywriter fills them, the Designer lays them out, and the Evaluator flags what needs a human decision. Pages stop arriving one at a time. Within the hour, thirty are waiting for her review, and more are coming down the line. The crew is fast. She is not. The handler who made the mission work is now the thing slowing it down. We stop on her facing the stack, and the question she has to answer next: how do you handle the bottleneck when the bottleneck is you?

Leave them knowing: For complex work, break the project into parts and give each one to a specialist sub-agent, with a person running the team and making the final call.
